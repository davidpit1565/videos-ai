## 0. What could and could not be obtained (read first)

- **YouTube first spoken sentence: NOT obtained for any video.** `yt-dlp --write-auto-subs` on a Shorts URL returned HTTP 429 and then "Sign in to confirm you're not a bot". I stopped there (no cookies, no workaround), as instructed. So for the YouTube rows the "first sentence" column holds the TITLE, labelled as such, and the spoken line is UNKNOWN.
- What did work: `yt-dlp --flat-playlist` on `youtube.com/hashtag/<tag>/shorts` (28 tags x up to 150 items) gives title and a rounded view count. It does NOT give duration or channel for hashtag feeds. `ytsearch60:` queries give duration and channel but returned almost only long videos (1,200 results, only 3 were Shorts-length with 1M+ views). Raw lists are in the scratchpad (`.../scratchpad/hooks/hc.txt`), not durable.
- The hashtag feeds are dominated by Hindi/Indonesian/Vietnamese, gadget-ad and comedy Shorts. I kept only phone/AI/privacy/app-relevant ones. They prove topic demand, not a hook style.
- Verbatim hook text for English videos came from hook libraries (Virlo, Hooked, TransClipper) and news articles. Virlo says it "checked the hook against what the creator said or showed on screen", so its text may be on-screen text rather than speech; I mark these "hook text (spoken or on-screen, not distinguished)". Every library is a vendor and only lists videos that already went viral (survivorship).
- Not obtained: any TikTok/Instagram page directly; follower counts for most rows; durations for most rows.
- tryvolume.app (measured 9.11.2026) lists tech hooks with multiples of the account's usual views, not absolute views, so those are NOT in the table (listed in section 2 as supporting evidence only).

## 2. The 8 hook templates that recur (and why, as hypotheses)

Source of the grouping: the table above plus Virlo's type statistics (2.4M hooks, updated 8.11.2026): POV hooks become outliers 29.6% of the time, shock 27.9%, comparison 27.1%, relatable 27.1%, question 22.1%, stop-doing-this 22.4%, bold claim and how-to about 19% (vendor figures; "outlier" is the vendor's own definition; no weights are published by platforms). The "why they work" text is my HYPOTHESIS, grounded in the evidence rules already in `hooks-guide.md` (felt problem, subject in the first words, testable in 30 seconds).

### T1. "Stop / Don't ... until you see this" (an instruction that protects something the viewer owns)
1. "Don't buy another charger until you see this." (row 30, 19.9M)
2. "DO NOT book another flight (before watching this)" (row 38, 14M)
3. "Stop posting from that plus sign if you wanna go viral." (row 39, 16.1M)
Why: names an everyday object in the first words, creates a possible loss, and the gap closes only if you watch. Works only if the thing is something the viewer uses weekly.

### T2. "Apple / they will never tell you" (a withheld secret about a product everyone owns)
1. "apple will never tell you this..." (row 42, 14.7M)
2. "Since TikTok is actually getting banned there's one more secret I have to share with you" (row 44, 26.8M)
3. "Apple Hid This in Plain Sight 😮" (row 11, 1.0M, title)
(Extra, views UNKNOWN: "Google doesn't want you to know this, but they've been allowing AI to scan every single one of your emails", Lori Greiner via UNILAD 7.5.2026; the claim itself was corrected by fact-checkers per the 9.11 viral file.)
Why: names the product in word one, implies the viewer is missing something they already own. Risk: the genre carries many exaggerated claims; we may only use it where we can check the claim.

### T3. PSA / "we were just made aware of a danger" (authority or personal failure, then one action)
1. "We're just made aware of a danger and it's for anybody that has an iPhone, so please listen up." (row 49, 2.7M+)
2. "I would like to make a PSA. I missed my original flight to Chicago this morning because I set an alarm on my iPhone ... and it went off, but silently." (row 48, 2.2M+)
3. "Did y'all know that Facebook Messenger, when you give the terms and conditions ... that when you connect to a Wi-Fi, they can go into that Wi-Fi map..." (row 50, 4.8M)
Why: the viewer hears a stake and a product before any setup; a real person's failure makes it feel true. Warning: two of three carried unverified or corrected mechanisms.

### T4. "If [annoyance you have lived], [here is the fix]" (relatable, then the remedy)
1. "If your car somehow only has wired Carplay, but the tiniest touch to the cord will completely disconnect it, you might just want to switch to one of these before the discount ends." (row 36, 28.4M)
2. "Listen, if you don't got AC in your car and you don't want to spend $500 to fix it, watch this." (row 37, 18.7M)
3. "Hi, so, you know how all of us collectively take all of our selfies on Snapchat or on Instagram stories because we all look ugly in the Apple camera because it flips our photos?" (TikTok @taliamar, 1.3M+)
Why: the first clause is a moment the viewer has lived, so no persuasion is needed; the remedy is one action. These are ad-style (the first two sell a product), which is why they are long; our version drops the sale.

### T5. Ultra-short text-first hook (1-5 words, a noun or contrast)
1. "Forgetting something?" (Aubrium reel, 6.4M)
2. "Exams might be doomed" (Aubrium reel, 6.3M)
3. "Regular alarm / Silent alarm" (row 35, 7.1M)
Why: HYPOTHESIS: a big on-screen phrase plus a striking visual does the stopping; Aubrium reports 1-4 word hooks had the highest follower multiples (vendor, survivorship). For us the first-second rule still applies: the short text must name the product and topic.

### T6. "Make the AI do something odd" (a test or a surprise from a named AI or a comedy of it)
1. "Asking chatgpt to speak cat for us" (row 29, 47.4M)
2. "How to Trick ChatGPT in 15 Seconds - Fooling AI" (row 9, 1.1M, title)
3. "Interviewer: tell me about yourself. AI: 😆" (row 23, 41M, title)
Why: a scene the viewer can picture in one second with a named AI; sendable as "look what it did". These are ChatGPT-centred and thus out for our candidates; the template applies to other products.

### T7. "[Named moment/product] + [count]" (a list promise attached to a named product or free offer)
1. "You just got a new MacBook. Here's three apps you need to download right away." (Aubrium reel, 4.4M)
2. "Not one, not two, not three, not four, not five, not six, but seven ways to charge your devices." (row 46, 5.3M)
3. "Claude Code Is Now Completely Free" / "7 Free AI Tools by Google" (creator-reported, 2.8M and 4.9M; titles)
Why: tells the viewer exactly what they get and for whom; a saveable list. Needs real running of each item (our "never promise what we did not test").

### T8. A specific named claim about an AI result or a person (credential or superlative, no setup)
1. "Two Polish engineers just built the most realistic AI voice in the world." (row 31, 29M)
2. "I run a 30 person creative agency and here are three agents that I've set up within Claude Cowork that run by themselves." (row 33, 26.3M)
3. "Have you ever wondered who the voice actor was behind this voice?" (Aubrium reel, 12M; a question with a specific gap)
Why: one concrete fact or question in the first clause, as `hooks-guide.md` already demands. Needs a claim we can check; the first two are impressive and not checkable by a stranger in 30 seconds.

- First frame: big drawn Photos search bar with the word "password" typed, one drawn photo thumbnail of a sticky note with a blurred scribble; iPhone Photos icon (real logo) top-left.
- Drawn: three result tiles (a sticky note, a Wi-Fi label on a router, an ID card, all illustrated, not real photos), a lock icon, then the setting path as a saveable card.
- Claim and evidence: Apple Support (support.apple.com/en-us/120004, dated 1.5.2026) says Live Text "recognizes information within your photos, videos, and images". An Apple Communities answer says Photos finds pictures by text inside them (a user answer, not an Apple document). UNCONFIRMED: that Photos search returns text matches on the current iOS, and where the processing happens. Do not claim "on device" or "uploaded" until Apple's own page is read.
- Overlap: Google photos is episode 37 (different company); 77 is hidden map pin. None is the same.
- Honest AI note: text recognition (OCR) is machine learning; call it "reads the text", not "AI looked at you".
- Self-score: HOOK 86, VIRALITY 82, OVERALL 82. Reason: imperative test in 8 words, personal, 10-second check; the weak spot is "AI" is implicit and not unusual.
